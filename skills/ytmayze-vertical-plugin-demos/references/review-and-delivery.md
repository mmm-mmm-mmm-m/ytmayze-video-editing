# Review and delivery

## Compare a real revision

Take feedback against a named cut. For a crop, gesture, or timing note, a short narrated screen recording with a circle, arrow, or typed label can replace several screenshots. Ask for the version, playback position, region, intended change, and anything the editor should preserve. Keep the recording and its transcript with the project. Derive one timestamped cue per distinct request, including the marked region. A transcript without the visual context can lose which panel or part of a gesture the creator means.

Use matched time ranges, camera positions, and source audio to isolate a disputed change. For a screen twitch or brightness pulse, compare the native capture, prepared crop, composition render, encoded file, and player in order. Inspect the stationary grid separately from the moving playhead or animated content. Sample the panel's geometry and brightness separately from the surrounding canvas. Apply the fix at the first stage where the problem appears, then replay the repair with its lead-in and return.

## Check the upload surface

1. Inspect the actual exported frame dimensions, display aspect ratio, rotation, and clean-aperture or file crop metadata when present. Decode the final file and inspect the intended shot. A correctly encoded 9:16 frame can still display with side gutters removed in a taller portrait player.
2. Match a posted or draft screenshot to the same timecode and playback surface. Identify the picture bounds and visible crop before placing the interface overlays. For a centered cover simulation, use the larger of the viewport-to-source width and height ratios. Compute the visible source rectangle from that scale. Use observed anchors to check alignment when the player crop, player bounds, or centering are unclear. Keep the crop and interface masks distinct. A feed, grid, and full-screen player can show the same post differently.
3. Check the actual shots at phone size. Include the deepest zoom, panel transitions, the last frame of a drag, important labels and controls, and the CTA. Keep breathing room around key content after crop and under the action rail and caption. A current platform template is a starting point. Record whether the result was checked with a simulation, draft, or processed upload on the target device. A draft preview can confirm framing while leaving transcoding unverified.

For HDR delivery, identify the project’s visually approved recipe and a known-good master. Compare the source transform, grade and gain, pixel format, color primaries, transfer and matrix, range, and applicable HDR metadata after final audio muxing. When investigating a display mismatch, make an SDR control and an HDR candidate from the same composition and compare matched frames in the intended phone player. Check neutral UI, highlights, gradients, and legibility. Local metadata validates the file. Playback after processing is the evidence for how the platform presents it. Use a private upload only when the user has authorized one. Label the platform check as pending when unavailable.

## Learn from a trial

Record which cut was posted, the platform and surface, the exposure window, and what else changed, such as caption, posting time, or audience. Treat counts from unmatched posts as observations. Use them to pick a baseline and nominate the next comparison. When available, compare early hold, reveal retention, finishes or replays, and actions that matter to the release. Try one meaningful creative change at a time with the same footage or mix where practical. Keep your causal explanation provisional when several choices changed together.

Finish by watching the full file on mute and with sound. Listen on a representative phone speaker through the hook, reveal, clicks, and CTA. Separately record the checks done on the source, editable project, encoded file, and platform playback. Frame counts, audio peaks, and transcripts can catch technical errors. They leave listening, creative acceptance, and real upload behavior to their corresponding reviews.
